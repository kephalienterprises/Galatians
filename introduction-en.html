Study Notes on Galatians

Introduction

Galatia and the Galatians

Greek and Latin authors use the terms Celtæ, Galatæ, and Galli without clear discrimination. [Burton]

In 390 BC, Gauls invaded Italy and captured Rome. Polybius called these Gauls Γαλάται and Κελτοί, and their country Γαλατία. [Burton]

After first overrunning the whole peninsula, the Gauls were defeated around 239 BC by Attalus I, king of Pergamum. As a result of this defeat, they were confined to a territory somewhat north and east of the center, bounded on the north by Bithynia and Paphlagonia, on the east by Pontus, on the south by Cappadocia and Lycaonia, and on the west by Phrygia. [Burton]

In 189 BC, this eastern Gaul, called by the Greeks Galatia or Gallo-Græcia, shared the fate of the rest of Asia Minor and came under the power of the Romans. [Burton]

In the first century BC, the region of Galatia was expanded. [Burton]

This results in a double use of the term Galatia. It can refer to the Roman territory in Asia Minor or, in the older ethnographic sense, to the more limited region. [Burton]

Galatia as a Roman province would include, besides the country properly so called, Lycaonia, Pisidia, the southeastern district of Phrygia, and a portion of Isauria... “Lycaonia” is especially mentioned as belonging to it, and there is evidence that the cities of Derbe and Lystra in particular were included within its boundaries. [J. B. Lightfoot]

More than six centuries after the Gauls, or Celts, had settled in Asia Minor, they had still preserved their language, even to the time of Jerome. [J. B. Lightfoot]

Where were the Galatian churches

The history of opinion

Ancient interpreters took it for granted without discussion that the churches were in the northern, Gallic, part of the province. The southern Galatian view was first proposed by J. J. Schmidt in 1788. [Burton]
